<h3 id="description">Description</h3>
<p>This course is an introduction to different theories of analysis in
the field of media studies. The readings and lectures trace the
development of humanities-based media theory from the 1950s through
today. The course introduces students to theories about authorship,
genre, audiences, media institutions, identity, race, class, gender, and
digital cultures. Students apply these theories to films, television
programs, and web-based media.</p>
<h3 id="course-goals">Course Goals</h3>
<ol type="1">
<li>For students to explain the development of media studies from the
1950s through today</li>
<li>For students to illustrate the different ways that media scholars
have used theory to categorize media texts, to analyze race and
representation, and to study media audiences</li>
<li>For students to comprehend and paraphrase complex media theory</li>
<li>For students to be able to apply different theories to media texts
and generate different meanings</li>
<li>For students to outline how broader intellectual movements and
traditions such as neo-formalism, Marxism, feminism, gender studies,
cultural studies, modernism, postmodernism, psychoanalysis, critical
race studies, and digital cultural studies influenced the development of
media studies.</li>
<li>For students to produce effective papers that involve summarizing a
theoretical article and applying it to a film, television, or web-based
media text</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="course-objectives">Course Objectives</h3>
<ol type="1">
<li>After completing this course students will have a broad
understanding of the development of media theory, and they can draw on
different areas of it for future classes</li>
<li>After completing this class students will understand how to read
theoretical articles for advanced COBF classes and for their own
independent research for future COBF classes</li>
<li>After completing this class students will comprehend there is no one
right interpretation of a text and that different theories can generate
different meanings for the same text</li>
<li>After completing this class students will be able to utilize media
theory to be critically literate media viewers and media producers</li>
<li>After completing this course students will be able to use their
understanding of diversity, equity, and inclusion issues in media
studies in future courses and in their future consumption of media</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="assessment">Assessment</h3>
<ul>
<li>25% attendance</li>
<li>25% weekly reading quizzes in-class</li>
<li>32% in-class essay parts (4 total)</li>
<li>8% midterm media artifact presentation</li>
<li>10% final in-class presentation</li>
</ul>
<p>The last three items here (50% of your grade in total) are all part
of the same larger project: an essay that you will draft in class across
four parts, with each part applying a media theory concept to a media
artifact (film, episode, publication, game, song, etc.) of your choice.
You should use the <em>same artifact</em> across most of these
exercises, though you may switch to a different artifact for one of the
four. You will give a brief, informal, in-class presentation in week 7
or 8 about your main artifact and what your analysis is turning up so
far. For our finals week session, I will ask you to review my feedback
on all your essay parts, think about how your different analyses fit
together, and present once more on what your artifact shows us about
media theory.</p>
<h3 id="laptopdevice-policy">Laptop/device policy</h3>
<p>Research on learning habits, as well as my own experience, supports
the idea that for most classrooms, most of the time, smartphones and
laptops do more to distract from learning than they do to aid it. I ask
that you please bring paper-and-pen notetaking materials to class with
you. I will not, as a general rule, allow laptop or phone use during
class time. If your device usage is causing problems after a friendly
reminder, I will mark your attendance as late or absent. This policy,
like most others here, is adaptable; I am eager to hear from you about
your access needs and thoughts on personal media devices.</p>
<h3 id="reading">Reading</h3>
<p>Our textbook for this course is <em>The Media Studies Toolkit</em> by
Michael Z. Newman (Routledge, 2022). I recommend that you purchase a
paperback copy, which should be available through the bookstore and
should cost $50 new. A digital version is available to you for free
through the library; if you do not buy the print book, please consider
printing out chapters as we read them so that you can refer to the text
in class. I will share other readings as PDFs through our Canvas page. I
am happy to print these for you if you let me know ahead of time. The
bulk of your work outside class time will be in reading, so please take
some time to think about—and check back in on—the reading setups and
strategies that work well for you!</p>
